    How TCP Congestion Control Works

    A sender on the internet cannot know the exact capacity of every switch, router, and optical cable along its path. To solve this, TCP obeys two fundamental guardrails:

    • Flow Control (rwnd): The receiver advertises its available buffer space in the header of every ACK packet. The sender guarantees that total bytes in flight never exceed rwnd, preventing a fast sender from crashing a slow consumer.
    • Congestion Control (cwnd): The sender dynamically models network capacity. It begins conservatively in Slow Start (doubling its window every RTT), transitions to linear growth (+1 segment per RTT) in Congestion Avoidance once it reaches ssthresh, and cuts back upon detecting packet loss.

    Additive Increase, Multiplicative Decrease (AIMD)

    In TCP Reno (RFC 5681), when a packet drop is detected via triple duplicate ACKs, TCP triggers Fast Retransmit and Fast Recovery: it sets ssthresh = max(2, cwnd / 2) and cuts cwnd in half. By climbing linearly (+1 MSS) and dropping multiplicatively (÷2), competing TCP flows mathematically converge to fair share bandwidth allocation while maximizing overall link throughput, creating the signature sawtooth waveform.

    Bufferbloat: When Queues Get Too Large

    Network routers maintain packet buffers to absorb temporary traffic bursts. However, if the router queue is excessively large compared to the Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP), TCP will continue additive increase, filling the buffer with dozens of segments before dropping a packet. This phenomenon—bufferbloat—causes massive round-trip latency spikes and jitter without improving throughput.
